# Signal Forge studio craft standard v3

Status: target release standard for premium Shorts and long-form. This document
raises the observable craft floor; existing rights, truth, resource, and release
arbiter blockers remain absolute.

## 1. Definition of a finished episode

A finished episode is not narration over background footage. It is one coherent
piece of direction in which research, words, performance, footage, typography,
animation, VFX, sound, and pacing all serve the same dramatic progression.

The autonomous studio may hold a story indefinitely. It may not publish a
placeholder voice, generic visual bed, static slideshow, copied edit, broken
generated shot, unfinished payoff, or render that merely passes codec checks.

## 2. Required preproduction packet

No timeline begins without:

- research dossier and claim graph;
- story thesis and viewer promise;
- source-backed talking points in causal order;
- beat sheet with tension, promise, escalation, mechanism, payoff, and echo;
- visual thesis: one sentence describing what the episode should look and feel
  like;
- shot list with purpose, duration target, visual layer, and required asset;
- voice-performance direction;
- music and sound-event map;
- rights/provenance status for every planned external asset;
- package hypothesis and experiment variable.

## 3. Talking-point standard

Every talking point contains:

```text
CLAIM -> EVIDENCE -> VISUAL -> CONSEQUENCE -> NEXT QUESTION
```

The script critic removes points that merely define a term, restate the title,
repeat an earlier claim, speculate without labeling, or cannot be shown. A Short
normally carries three to five points. Long-form acts contain multiple points,
but each act must produce a state change or answer a question raised earlier.

Technical language is kept when it makes the story more specific. It is
translated through consequences and visuals, not replaced with empty analogies.

## 4. Visual architecture

Every shot has one primary job:

- **Evidence:** prove what happened with a receipt, artifact, interface, document,
  measurement, or permissioned source clip.
- **Explanation:** make a hidden mechanism visible through an original diagram,
  simulation, reconstruction, map, timeline, or comparison.
- **Emotion:** communicate scale, danger, speed, isolation, awe, absurdity, or
  relief through cinematography, performance, or designed atmosphere.

Generic footage may establish atmosphere but may never impersonate evidence.
“Background footage” cannot be the entire visual plan. The visual spine must
change with the story.

### Shorts floor

- 12–20 intentional shots for a typical 30–55 second episode;
- three hero moments: stop, escalation/reversal, and payoff;
- at least two source-specific proof moments;
- at least three original motion/VFX treatments;
- no unchanged composition longer than two seconds unless marked as a dramatic
  hold and supported by sound/performance;
- mobile-safe hierarchy with one dominant object and one caption idea;
- Reddit gameplay, when used, is kinetic support only and receives purposeful
  crops, speed changes, cuts, focus treatment, and story-synced overlays.

### Long-form floor

- an episode-specific opening sequence, not a reusable logo intro;
- a new visual mode or meaningful compositional change at every argument turn;
- recurring visual motifs that evolve with the thesis;
- original diagrams/simulations for invisible mechanisms;
- source evidence shown long enough to understand, with guided focus;
- deliberate quiet or longer shots only when the emotional beat earns them.

## 5. Footage system

The footage engine searches only approved, traceable sources and the cataloged
library. It selects by semantic shot need, not broad topic keyword.

Every candidate clip records creator, URL, permission basis, commercial and
derivative rights, attribution, checksum, technical quality, and allowed use.
The selector scores relevance to the exact talking point, composition, motion,
resolution, continuity, uniqueness, and prior reuse count.

The engine rejects:

- clips without explicit permission records;
- repeated filler used because it is available;
- unrelated “person typing,” server racks, matrix code, glowing chips, or random
  city footage;
- watermarks, burned captions, logos that cannot be used, severe compression,
  or framing that cannot survive 9:16;
- footage that visually claims a real event it does not document.

When no honest footage exists, build an original animation, reconstruction, or
simulation and label it when viewers could mistake it for documentation.

## 6. Motion graphics and VFX grammar

The studio uses a restrained set of high-quality capabilities, combined in
episode-specific ways:

- tracked callouts and interface focus;
- screen replacement and clean device compositing;
- 2.5D parallax from cleared stills;
- depth, rack-focus, controlled camera motion, and motivated zooms;
- maps, timelines, dependency graphs, counters, charts, code/data transforms,
  and before/after states;
- particles, light, volumetrics, fracture, distortion, and environmental effects
  only when they represent a real beat or emotion;
- Blender hero shots for scale, machinery, devices, networks, and impossible
  perspectives;
- Manim/Remotion simulations for algorithms, attacks, queues, model behavior,
  infrastructure, and measured comparisons;
- typography used as choreography, not a permanent caption wall.

No effect is added only to look expensive. Every effect must clarify evidence,
reveal mechanism, intensify consequence, transition between causal states, or
land the payoff. The critic rejects default presets, random transitions,
continuous camera shake, overused glitch, illegible speed, fake interfaces, and
effects that hide weak source material.

## 7. Caption design

Captions are part of the composition. They use two to five words per burst,
phrase-aware timing, one purposeful emphasis, measured text fit, and shot-aware
placement. They may disappear when a source receipt or visual needs the frame.

Caption animation follows speech energy and meaning. Default word bouncing,
karaoke on every syllable, giant all-caps blocks, and permanent yellow text are
rejected. Every approved word is covered and aligned to final audio.

## 8. Voice and performance

The voice sounds like a technically literate person who cannot wait to show the
viewer what happened. It has intention, contrast, pace changes, disbelief,
restraint, and a real landing on the payoff.

The performance packet marks stress, pace, pronunciation, emotional turn,
breath, and intentional silence by phrase. Utility TTS is never public. A neural
voice must pass pronunciation, back-transcription, naturalness, fatigue, and
rights checks. Failed premium synthesis blocks release; it does not silently
fall back.

## 9. Music, SFX, and sonic identity

Each episode receives separate voice, music, ambience, transition, and impact
stems. Sound events are mapped before the edit:

- source-relevant cold-open sound or immediate speech;
- subtle pulse or texture under unresolved tension;
- tactile UI/mechanical sounds tied to visible actions;
- risers only for a real escalation;
- impact or controlled silence on reversal and payoff;
- an ending sound that closes or loops the opening idea.

Random whooshes, nonstop impacts, meme sounds without editorial purpose, and
music that dictates the same arc in every episode are rejected. All sound is
rights-cleared. Final mix remains -16 to -14 LUFS with true peak at or below
-1 dBTP, intelligible on phone speakers and headphones.

## 10. Color, texture, and finish

Signal Forge uses a recognizable base system—type family, spacing, proof labels,
caption behavior, and sonic mark—without making every episode visually
identical. Each story receives a controlled palette, material, lighting logic,
and motion motif derived from its world.

Final review checks grade continuity, black/white levels, skin tones when
present, banding, aliasing, compression, motion blur, temporal consistency,
generated artifacts, duplicate frames, and accidental low-resolution assets.

## 11. Autonomous creative organization

The pipeline separates responsibilities even when models are local:

1. **News editor:** finds and ranks timely candidates.
2. **Researcher:** builds the dossier and claim graph.
3. **Story producer:** chooses thesis, talking points, and format.
4. **Director:** writes the beat sheet, visual thesis, and shot list.
5. **Asset producer:** acquires or creates every approved shot.
6. **Voice director:** creates and checks the performed narration.
7. **Picture editor:** assembles timing, footage, motion, and captions.
8. **Sound editor:** creates the mix and sonic arc.
9. **Packaging editor:** proposes titles, thumbnails, and first frames.
10. **Independent critic:** grades observable evidence and orders at most two
    targeted revisions.
11. **Release controller:** verifies rights, policy, privacy, checksum, schedule,
    and idempotency.

The producer cannot approve its own output. Missing evidence fails closed. A
revision names the exact failing beat, shot, phrase, mix event, or package; it
does not regenerate the whole episode blindly.

## 12. Final cut gate

A public candidate must answer yes with evidence:

- Is the first frame unmistakably about this story?
- Does the first line create tension within 100 ms?
- Is the promise clear by 2.5 seconds?
- Does every talking point advance the causal story?
- Are the best available receipts shown and readable?
- Are the VFX technically clean and editorially motivated?
- Does the visual spine change with the story rather than decorate narration?
- Is the voice performed rather than read?
- Does sound create contrast without masking speech?
- Is the payoff stronger than the setup and fully delivered?
- Is the title/thumbnail/first-frame promise accurate?
- Are all claims, footage, music, fonts, models, and generated assets cleared?
- Does the encoded file pass dimensions, duration, captions, loudness, artifact,
  and resource checks?
- Would removing any shot, sentence, effect, or sound make no difference? If so,
  remove it.
